P/E Ratio vs P/B Ratio: When to Use Which Stock Valuation Metric

Is a stock cheap because its P/E is 12, or is it a dangerous value trap? Learn how professional equity research analysts combine P/E and P/B ratios across different sectors.

⚡ Executive Summary

Quick Answer & Overview

P/E compares market price to annual earnings per share (best for asset-light FMCG, IT, and Pharma). P/B compares price to book value per share (mandatory for capital-intensive Banks, NBFCs, and Steel). Connecting both via RoE exposes hidden value traps.

📌 Key Takeaways

  • Asset-Light Stocks: Use P/E for companies driven by brand power and high intellectual property (IT, FMCG, Consumer).
  • Asset-Heavy Stocks: Use P/B for financial institutions (Banks, NBFCs) and capital-intensive cyclical sectors (Steel, Cement).
  • The DuPont Connection: P/B equals P/E multiplied by Return on Equity (P/B = P/E × RoE).
  • Value Trap Warning: A low P/B ratio under 1.0 often signals hidden non-performing assets (NPAs) or unviable business models.

🎯 What You'll Learn

How to avoid valuation traps and select the right metric for banking stocks vs tech giants.

Why Applying a Single Valuation Metric Fails

Beginning stock investors often fall into the trap of using a single metric—like low Price-to-Earnings (P/E)—to screen for 'cheap' stocks. However, comparing a tech giant like Tata Consultancy Services (TCS) to a public sector lender like State Bank of India (SBI) using P/E alone is fundamentally flawed.

TCS does not need massive factories or physical capital to double its revenues. Its value comes from intellectual property, client contracts, and human talent. SBI's earnings, on the other hand, depend entirely on the size and quality of its loan book (assets) funded by customer deposits (liabilities).

When to Use Price-to-Earnings (P/E) Ratio

The P/E ratio measures how much investors are willing to pay for every ₹1 of net annual profit generated by the company. It is the premier valuation tool for asset-light, high-margin, and consumer-facing sectors including:

  • Information Technology (IT Services): TCS, Infosys, HCL Tech.
  • Fast-Moving Consumer Goods (FMCG): Hindustan Unilever, Nestlé India, Britannia.
  • Pharmaceuticals & Speciality Chemicals: Sun Pharma, Divi's Labs.

For these companies, balance sheet book value is tiny relative to their cash generation. Paying 30x P/E for a business growing earnings at 20% annually with zero debt is often far safer than buying a 5x P/E company whose earnings are collapsing.

When to Use Price-to-Book (P/B) Ratio

The P/B ratio compares a company's market capitalization to its Net Asset Value (Book Value = Total Assets minus Total Liabilities). P/B is mandatory for financial institutions and capital-heavy cyclical industries:

  • Banking & NBFCs: HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank, Bajaj Finance. (For banks, P/BV evaluates the price paid per rupee of net loan assets).
  • Commodities & Capital Goods: Tata Steel, Hindalco, Larsen & Toubro.

The Golden Link: Connecting P/E and P/B via RoE

Professional equity analysts connect both metrics using Return on Equity (RoE):

Price to Book (P/B) = Price to Earnings (P/E) × Return on Equity (RoE)

This mathematical identity explains why a bank with a high RoE of 18% deserves to trade at 3.0x P/B, while a bank with a dismal 4% RoE deserves to trade at a discount of 0.6x P/B.

Accounting Principles, Adjustments & Regulatory Nuances

Evaluating P/E Ratio vs P/B Ratio requires delving deeper into financial statement analysis, accounting standards (Ind AS / IFRS), and corporate reporting quality:

  • Quality of Earnings & Cash Flow Validation: Reported net profits can be distorted by non-cash items, one-off asset sales, or aggressive revenue recognition policies. Comparing reported operating profit against actual Operating Cash Flow (OCF) reveals whether earnings are backed by hard cash collection.
  • Balance Sheet Health & Off-Balance Sheet Liabilities: Inspecting footnotes for contingent liabilities, pending tax litigation, corporate guarantees, and lease liabilities under Ind AS 116 ensures that solvency metrics reflect true financial obligations.
  • Working Capital Efficiency: Analyzing the Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC = Days Inventory Outstanding + Days Sales Outstanding - Days Payable Outstanding) shows how fast a company converts raw material investments back into cash in bank.

Comparative Industry Benchmarks & Strategic Valuation Analysis

Financial ratios cannot be evaluated in isolation. Contextualizing metrics against historical medians and sector benchmarks exposes valuation dislocations:

  • High ROIC vs Low Cost of Capital: Companies that consistently generate Return on Invested Capital (ROIC) significantly above their Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC) create economic value and deserve premium valuation multiples.
  • Detecting Financial Value Traps: A company trading at low valuation multiples may appear attractive, but deteriorating interest coverage ratios, rising bad loans, or technological obsolescence signal value trap risks.

5-Step Analytical Framework for Investors & Analysts

  • Review 5-year historical trend lines for revenue growth, operating margin, and return metrics.
  • Cross-verify reported earnings against Cash Flow from Operations (CFO).
  • Compare key valuation metrics against direct industry competitors and 10-year median averages.
  • Screen balance sheets for debt-to-equity ratios and contingent liability footnotes.
  • Stress-test earnings forecasts against economic downturns and raw material price spikes.

P/E for Asset-Light Tech vs P/B for Capital-Intensive Banks

Valuation multiples are only meaningful when applied to the correct industry business models:

  • Price-to-Earnings (P/E) for Asset-Light Companies: Best suited for IT services (TCS, Infosys), consumer staples, and SaaS firms where physical book value is minimal, and earnings power is driven by intellectual property and brand equity.
  • Price-to-Book (P/B) for Financials & Capital-Intensive Sectors: P/B is the gold standard for commercial banks, NBFCs, and real estate developers. A bank's balance sheet consists primarily of loans and liquid deposits, making Book Value per Share (BVPS) the primary anchor for fair valuation.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a stock have a negative P/E ratio?

When a company reports a net loss, its P/E ratio becomes negative. Financial databases typically display negative P/E as 'N/A' or 'Not Meaningful'.

Why do banks use Price-to-Adjusted Book Value (P/ABV)?

Adjusted Book Value subtracts Net Non-Performing Assets (NPAs) from reported book value to show true tangible capital backing.

What is a PEG ratio?

PEG ratio is P/E divided by annual EPS growth rate. A PEG under 1.0 suggests a stock may be undervalued relative to its growth rate.

Is a low P/E stock always better than a high P/E stock?

No. Low P/E stocks often reflect low growth, poor corporate governance, or declining industry prospects.

What is a good P/B ratio for Indian banks?

Quality private banks with 16%+ RoE trade between 2.5x and 3.5x P/B. Stressed lenders trade below 1.0x P/B.
