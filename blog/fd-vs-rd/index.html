Fixed Deposit (FD) vs Recurring Deposit (RD): Which Is Better?

FD and RD are both familiar bank-deposit options. The key difference is not which one is universally better; it is whether your money is available now or arrives gradually each month.

Quick answer

A fixed deposit puts one lump sum away for a chosen tenure. A recurring deposit builds savings through regular instalments. An FD can suit money already available; an RD can suit a monthly saving habit. Compare rate, tenure, withdrawal rules and tax before deciding.

Key takeaways

  • FD starts with one deposit
  • RD starts with regular deposits
  • Terms vary between institutions

What you’ll learn

How interest, liquidity, flexibility and inflation change the practical choice.

What is an FD?

A fixed deposit is a one-time deposit kept for a specified tenure. The institution quotes a rate and maturity value under its terms. You might use an FD after receiving a bonus, selling an asset, or setting aside money for a known goal. The full principal begins earning interest immediately.

What is an RD?

A recurring deposit accepts a fixed contribution at regular intervals, commonly each month. It can suit someone who earns monthly and wants a defined saving routine. Each instalment has less time to earn than the earlier instalments, so an RD and an FD with the same total contribution do not produce the same maturity amount.

Worked examples

₹5 lakh FD for five years

At an illustrative 7% annual rate compounded annually, ₹5 lakh becomes roughly ₹7.01 lakh after five years. The exact maturity depends on the institution’s compounding method, quoted rate and product terms. Use the FD Calculator to test different tenures and rates.

₹5,000 monthly RD for five years

Contributions total ₹3 lakh. At a similar illustrative rate, maturity may be around ₹3.55–₹3.60 lakh, because the first deposit earns for almost five years while the final one earns for only a short period. The RD Calculator makes that timing visible.

Interest, liquidity and tax

Deposit interest is usually calculated and credited according to the product terms. Do not assume every bank uses identical rates, compounding intervals or premature-closure penalties. Liquidity is also relative: an FD may be closed early, but doing so can reduce the interest earned. An RD may penalise missed instalments or have closure rules.

Interest can be taxable, and rules depend on the current law and your circumstances. For a personal decision, compare the after-tax return and purchasing power, not only the headline rate. Inflation can make a growing balance worth less in real terms; use the Inflation Calculator to explore the effect.

Advantages and limitations

FD advantages

  • Entire amount begins earning immediately
  • Simple maturity planning
  • Useful for a defined lump-sum goal

RD advantages

  • Fits regular income
  • Builds a saving habit
  • No need for a large amount up front

FD can be inconvenient if you need the money unexpectedly. RD can be inconvenient if monthly income is irregular. Neither is designed to guarantee that returns beat inflation after tax.

Expert tip

Match tenure to the date you need the money. Avoid choosing a long lock-in solely for a slightly higher rate if an emergency could force a premature withdrawal.

Decision checklist

  • Do I have a lump sum now, or am I saving from monthly income?
  • When will I need this money?
  • What happens if I withdraw early or miss an RD instalment?
  • What is the after-tax, inflation-adjusted result?
  • Have I kept an accessible emergency buffer separate?

Warning

A deposit rate is not the same as a guaranteed improvement in purchasing power. Review inflation, tax and liquidity before committing money to a tenure.

Myth versus fact

Myth: an RD is just an FD paid monthly. Fact: each RD instalment gets a different amount of time to earn interest. Myth: the highest rate is automatically best. Fact: early-closure rules, cash needs and after-tax return matter too. For broader growth illustrations, see the Compound Interest Calculator and SIP vs Lumpsum Guide.

Summary

Choose FD when a suitable lump sum is already available for a known period. Choose RD when regular contributions are the realistic route to that goal. The useful decision is the one that protects necessary liquidity while giving planned savings a clear home.

Frequently asked questions

Can an FD be renewed?

Many institutions offer renewal options; check the rate and instructions at maturity.

Can I increase an RD instalment?

Product rules differ; some may require a new RD.

Which has better liquidity?

Both may have early-closure routes, but the exact terms decide the practical answer.

Are senior-citizen rates different?

Some institutions offer different rates; verify the current terms.

Does RD interest compound?

It is calculated according to the deposit’s stated terms and instalment timing.

Can I use FD for emergency money?

Consider access needs and withdrawal penalties before relying on it.

How do I compare returns?

Compare maturity value, tenure, liquidity, tax and inflation—not only the rate.

Is an RD better than a SIP?

They serve different products and risk profiles; compare the goal and risk tolerance.

What if I miss an RD payment?

Check the institution’s penalty and continuation rules.

Should I choose the longest tenure?

Only if it matches when you need the money and the terms suit you.
