Getting Started

Introduction

Every time money crosses a border, an exchange rate determines how much the recipient receives in their local currency. Yet most people sending money internationally have only a vague understanding of how exchange rates work — and this knowledge gap costs real money.

Banks and many money transfer services generate significant revenue from the gap between the exchange rate they source on wholesale markets and the rate they offer to retail customers. Understanding this gap is the single most important step to reducing the cost of an international money transfer.

This guide explains exchange rates from the ground up — what they are, how they move, how providers price currency, and what you can do to make better-informed transfer decisions.

Foundations

What Is an Exchange Rate?

An exchange rate is the price at which one currency can be converted into another. It expresses how many units of a second currency you receive for one unit of a first currency.

Exchange rates are quoted as a currency pair. For example, if the GBP/USD rate is quoted as 1.27, it means one British pound buys 1.27 US dollars. The first currency in the pair (GBP) is the base currency, and the second (USD) is the quote currency.

Exchange rates fluctuate continuously during global trading hours. The foreign exchange market operates across multiple time zones simultaneously, making it one of the largest and most liquid financial markets in the world. Rates respond to economic data, political developments, and shifting market sentiment in real time.

Real-World Impact

Why Exchange Rates Matter

For most everyday consumers, the stated transfer fee is less significant than the exchange rate applied. A provider that charges no upfront fee but applies an unfavourable exchange rate may cost significantly more than one with a modest fee and a better rate.

This is because exchange-rate margin scales with the transfer amount. A rate that is less favourable by just one percent means a meaningful difference on a large transfer, even when the explicit fee is zero.

Understanding the exchange rate is also important for businesses. A company importing goods from another country and paying invoices in a foreign currency is directly exposed to exchange rate movements. A rate shift between the time a price is agreed and the time payment is made can materially affect profit margins.

Core Concept

The Mid-Market Exchange Rate

The mid-market rate — also called the interbank rate, spot rate, or real exchange rate — is the midpoint between the price at which a currency can be bought and the price at which it can be sold on international markets at any given moment.

It is the rate displayed on financial data platforms, currency converters, and financial news sites. It is the closest approximation to the "true" market rate. Banks trade with each other at or near this rate on the wholesale interbank market.

The gap between buying and selling rates is the spread — the provider's margin.

Retail customers — individuals and businesses — do not have access to the mid-market rate directly. Banks and money transfer services apply a margin to this rate, creating the spread. Providers that are transparent about this — showing both the rate used and the fee charged separately — make it easier for customers to understand the true cost of a transfer.

Rate Types

Buying Rate vs Selling Rate

Currency providers operate with two rates for every currency pair:

Buying Rate (Bid)

The rate at which the provider will purchase a foreign currency from you. This is the rate applied when you are exchanging foreign currency back into your home currency. It is lower than the mid-market rate.

Selling Rate (Ask/Offer)

The rate at which the provider will sell a foreign currency to you. This is the rate applied when you are converting your home currency into a foreign currency for a transfer. It is higher than the mid-market rate.

The difference between the buying rate and the selling rate is the bid-ask spread. The wider this spread, the more the provider earns from each currency exchange — and the more it costs you. Narrower spreads generally indicate more competitive pricing.

When comparing providers, you are effectively comparing the spread and any explicit fees. Providers that operate transparently separate these two elements, while others bundle them together in a single quoted rate.

Hidden Costs

Exchange Rate Margin

The exchange rate margin is the percentage by which a provider's offered rate differs from the mid-market rate. It is the primary mechanism through which many banks and transfer services generate revenue from currency exchange.

Unlike a stated fee, the margin is often invisible unless the customer compares the offered rate against an independent source like a financial data platform. This is why providers can advertise "no transfer fee" while still earning significantly from each transaction through the exchange rate alone.

Example: How the Margin Works

If the mid-market GBP/INR rate is 107.00, and a provider offers you 104.86, the difference of 2.14 represents a margin of approximately 2%. On a £5,000 transfer, that 2% margin costs you roughly £100 in rate terms — regardless of whether any explicit fee is also charged.

The most effective approach is to focus on the net payout — the actual amount that arrives in the recipient's account in the destination currency — rather than only comparing stated fees.

Building Blocks

Currency Pair Basics

Currency exchange is always expressed as a pair — you are always buying one currency by selling another. Each pair has a base currency and a quote currency. The exchange rate tells you how many units of the quote currency are needed to purchase one unit of the base currency.

Currency Pair Base Quote What It Means
GBP/USD British Pound US Dollar How many USD = 1 GBP
EUR/INR Euro Indian Rupee How many INR = 1 EUR
USD/JPY US Dollar Japanese Yen How many JPY = 1 USD
AUD/SGD Australian Dollar Singapore Dollar How many SGD = 1 AUD

Understanding which direction the quote runs matters when reading exchange rate displays. When you are sending money internationally, the rate you need is always expressed in terms of the destination currency per unit of your sending currency.

Major, Minor & Exotic Currencies

Major Pairs

USD + Major Currencies

Major pairs all involve the US dollar paired with another heavily traded currency. They have the highest liquidity and typically the tightest spreads.

Exotic pairs combine a major currency with an emerging or less-traded currency. They carry wider spreads, more volatility, and may have limited availability.

Rate Timing

Spot Rate vs Forward Rate

Spot Rate

The Current Market Rate

The spot rate is the prevailing exchange rate for immediate currency exchange. Settlement typically occurs within one or two business days. It reflects real-time market conditions and changes continuously during trading hours. Most consumer money transfers execute at or near the spot rate (with provider margin applied).

Forward Rate

A Future Exchange Rate

A forward rate is agreed today for a currency exchange that will settle at a specified future date. Forward rates are derived from the spot rate adjusted for the interest rate differential between the two currencies. They allow users to eliminate uncertainty about future exchange rates.

Forward rates and spot rates often differ slightly due to the interest rate differential — the difference in central bank rates between the two countries. A currency from a country with higher interest rates typically trades at a slight forward discount relative to a currency with lower interest rates.

For most personal transfers, the spot rate is sufficient. For businesses or individuals with future currency needs — such as completing a property purchase in three months — locking in a forward rate removes the uncertainty of where the market will be at that future date.

Risk Management

Forward Contracts

A forward contract is an agreement between a customer and a currency provider to exchange a specified amount of currency at a predetermined rate on a specified future date. Entering into a forward contract locks in the exchange rate regardless of how the market moves between now and settlement.

Practical Example

A UK buyer has agreed to purchase a property in Spain, completing in four months. The purchase price is €250,000. To protect against the euro strengthening against the pound before completion, the buyer enters a forward contract at the exchange rate. Whatever happens to the GBP/EUR rate over the next four months, the agreed amount in pounds is fixed.

Forward contracts are useful for:

  • Overseas property buyers whose purchase price is agreed in a foreign currency
  • Businesses paying international suppliers at fixed future payment dates
  • Companies pricing goods or services in foreign currencies and wanting to protect margins
  • Individuals receiving a future inheritance or settlement payment in a foreign currency

Most forward contracts require a deposit at the time of booking, with the balance settled on the future date. Not all money transfer providers offer forward contracts — specialist FX brokers are typically better equipped for this product than general-purpose transfer apps.

Alongside forward contracts, limit orders are another tool — these instruct a provider to automatically execute a transfer when the exchange rate reaches a specified target, allowing users to aim for a better rate without needing to monitor markets continuously.

Market Dynamics

Exchange Rate Volatility

Exchange rate volatility describes how much a rate fluctuates over a given period. High volatility means the rate can change rapidly and significantly — which increases the uncertainty around the value of future payments or receipts in a foreign currency.

Volatility differs significantly across currency pairs:

Category Typical Volatility Examples Spread Characteristics
Major Pairs Generally lower EUR/USD, GBP/USD Tighter spreads
Minor Pairs Moderate EUR/GBP, GBP/AUD Moderate spreads
Exotic Pairs Generally higher USD/INR, EUR/TRY Wider spreads

Periods of heightened volatility — often triggered by major economic announcements, central bank decisions, or geopolitical events — can cause exchange rates to move rapidly within a single day. For senders with timing flexibility, understanding when volatility tends to increase can inform the decision of when to transfer.

Market Drivers

Factors Affecting Exchange Rates

🏦 Interest Rates

Central bank interest rate decisions are among the most powerful drivers of exchange rates. Higher interest rates tend to attract foreign capital seeking better returns, increasing demand for that currency and pushing its value higher.

📈 Inflation

Countries with lower inflation rates generally see their currency appreciate over time, as purchasing power is preserved relative to higher-inflation countries. Inflation erodes the real value of a currency.

📊 Economic Data

Economic indicators — employment figures, GDP growth, manufacturing output, consumer confidence — affect market expectations about a country's economic health and therefore the value of its currency.

🌍 Political Stability

Political uncertainty, elections, policy changes, and geopolitical events can cause sudden currency movements. Markets tend to favour currencies from politically stable countries with consistent economic policy frameworks.

⚖️ Trade Balance

A country that exports more than it imports experiences higher demand for its currency (since foreign buyers need the local currency to pay for goods). Trade surpluses generally support currency strength.

🔮 Market Sentiment

Investor sentiment, risk appetite, and speculative positioning all influence exchange rates. During periods of global uncertainty, demand often flows toward perceived "safe haven" currencies such as the US dollar, Swiss franc, and Japanese yen.

Practical Guidance

How to Compare Exchange Rates

The most reliable method for comparing money transfer providers is to calculate the net payout — the exact amount the recipient will receive in the destination currency — for your specific transfer amount and currency pair on each platform. This is the single figure that reflects all costs combined.

1
Note the mid-market rate

Check the live mid-market rate on a financial data platform before comparing quotes. This gives you a benchmark.

2
Compare net payouts, not just fees

Enter your exact transfer amount on each platform and record the net destination amount before confirming. Do not focus only on the stated fee.

3
Account for the payment method

Funding a transfer by credit card often carries additional charges. Bank transfer or debit card funding is typically cheaper.

4
Consider delivery speed

Faster delivery options sometimes cost more. If timing is flexible, economy or standard options may offer better value.

5
Check for transfer limits

Some providers apply minimum or maximum transfer amounts. Verify these are compatible with your transfer size before registering.

Avoid These

Common Mistakes When Comparing Exchange Rates

Comparing fees without the rate: A transfer with no stated fee may still cost more than one with a visible fee if the rate margin is larger. Always compare net payouts.
Assuming the bank is the safest or most cost-effective option: Banks are regulated and safe, but their exchange rate margins are often among the widest. Safety and rate competitiveness are separate considerations.
Using a currency converter as the final reference: Currency converters display the mid-market rate. This is not the rate you will receive — it is a benchmark only.
Ignoring the payment method surcharge: Paying by credit card can significantly increase total transfer cost. Always check whether your chosen payment method carries additional charges.
Not comparing on the same transfer amount: Exchange rate margins may be tiered by transfer size. Always compare using your actual intended transfer amount, not a round figure that may attract a different rate tier.
Transferring urgently when timing is flexible: If a transfer does not need to arrive immediately, economy delivery options or waiting for a more favourable rate movement may reduce costs.
Action Guide

Practical Tips for Better Exchange Rates

✓ Compare the net payout

Always calculate the exact amount arriving in the destination currency across at least two or three providers before deciding.

✓ Set up rate alerts

If timing is flexible, rate alert tools allow you to be notified when a currency pair reaches a desired level — without monitoring markets constantly.

✓ Use limit orders for target rates

Limit order tools available through specialist FX providers automatically execute your transfer when the market rate reaches your specified target.

✓ Consider forward contracts for large future transfers

For significant future currency needs — property purchases, emigration funds, large invoices — a forward contract removes exchange rate uncertainty.

✓ Fund by bank transfer where possible

Bank transfer or debit card funding is generally cheaper than credit card funding, which may attract additional fees.

✓ Batch regular transfers where it makes sense

For recurring international payments, consolidating multiple smaller transfers into fewer, larger ones can reduce the impact of per-transaction fixed fees.

Key Terms

Exchange Rate Glossary

Term Definition
Mid-Market Rate The midpoint between the buy and sell price of a currency pair. Also called the interbank or real exchange rate.
Spread The difference between the buying and selling rates, or between the mid-market rate and the offered rate. Represents the provider's margin.
Spot Rate The exchange rate for immediate currency exchange, settling within one to two business days.
Forward Rate An exchange rate agreed now for settlement on a specified future date.
Forward Contract An agreement to exchange currency at a specified rate on a future date, locking in the rate regardless of market movements.
Limit Order An instruction to execute a currency transfer automatically when the market reaches a specified target rate.
Base Currency The first currency in a currency pair. One unit of the base currency is being priced in terms of the quote currency.
Quote Currency The second currency in a currency pair. The exchange rate expresses how many units of this currency equal one unit of the base currency.
Interbank Rate The rate at which banks trade currency with each other on wholesale markets. Equivalent to the mid-market rate.
Volatility The degree of fluctuation in an exchange rate over a given period. Higher volatility = more uncertainty in future rates.
Currency Risk The risk that adverse exchange rate movements will reduce the value of a transaction denominated in a foreign currency.
Bid Price The rate at which a provider buys currency from you. Always lower than the ask price.
Ask / Offer Price The rate at which a provider sells currency to you. Always higher than the bid price.
Common Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an exchange rate?
An exchange rate is the price at which one currency can be exchanged for another. It tells you how many units of a second currency you receive for one unit of a first currency. Exchange rates fluctuate continuously based on supply and demand in global currency markets.
What is the mid-market exchange rate?
The mid-market rate is the midpoint between the buy and sell prices of a currency on international markets. It is the rate used in financial data and currency converters. Most providers do not pass this rate directly to customers — they add a margin above it.
Why is the rate I receive different from the mid-market rate?
Banks and money transfer providers add a margin to the mid-market rate to cover their costs and generate revenue. The rate you receive is therefore less favourable than the raw mid-market rate. Understanding this spread is key to comparing the true cost of a transfer.
What is an exchange rate spread?
A spread is the difference between the exchange rate a provider offers and the mid-market rate. It is the built-in margin representing the provider's revenue from the currency conversion. A narrower spread means a better deal for the customer.
What is a spot rate?
A spot rate is the exchange rate available for immediate currency exchange and settlement, typically within one or two business days. It reflects the present state of the currency market and changes continuously during trading hours.
What is a forward contract?
A forward contract is an agreement to exchange currency at a specified rate on a future date. It allows individuals and businesses to lock in the exchange rate now, protecting against unfavourable movements before the transaction settles.
What is a limit order in currency exchange?
A limit order instructs a currency provider to execute a transfer automatically when the exchange rate reaches a specified target. It allows users to aim for a better rate than currently available without monitoring the market continuously.
What factors affect exchange rates?
Exchange rates are influenced by many factors including interest rates, inflation differentials, economic data releases such as employment and GDP figures, political events, trade balances, and overall market sentiment.
What is currency volatility?
Currency volatility refers to the degree to which an exchange rate fluctuates over a given period. High volatility means rapid and significant rate movements. Emerging market currencies tend to be more volatile than major pairs like EUR/USD.
How do banks price foreign currency?
Banks typically apply a markup above the mid-market rate and may also charge a flat fee per transfer. The combined effect of the spread and the fee determines the total cost. Bank foreign exchange rates are often less competitive than those offered by specialist money transfer providers.
What are major currency pairs?
Major currency pairs involve the US dollar paired with another widely traded currency such as EUR/USD, GBP/USD, USD/JPY, and USD/CHF. They are the most actively traded pairs and typically have tighter spreads.
What are exotic currency pairs?
Exotic pairs involve one major currency paired with the currency of an emerging or smaller economy — for example, USD/INR, EUR/TRY, or GBP/NGN. They tend to have wider spreads and more volatility.
Does the exchange rate I see on a currency app apply to my transfer?
No. Currency apps display the mid-market rate, which is not generally available to retail customers. Banks and transfer providers add a margin, so the rate you receive will be less favourable than the displayed market rate.
What is exchange rate risk?
Exchange rate risk is the possibility that a change in exchange rates will negatively affect the value of a transaction. For a business expecting a foreign currency payment, a fall in that currency's value before payment arrives could reduce the home-currency equivalent received.
How can I protect against exchange rate risk?
Common tools include forward contracts, which lock in a rate for a future transaction, and limit orders, which execute automatically at a desired rate. Whether these tools are appropriate depends on your situation and risk tolerance.
What is the difference between a spot rate and a forward rate?
A spot rate is the market exchange rate for immediate exchange. A forward rate is agreed today for currency exchange at a specified future date. Forward rates may differ from spot rates based on the interest rate differential between the two currencies.
What is a currency margin?
A currency margin is the percentage difference between the mid-market rate and the rate offered to you. A margin on a large transfer can represent a significant sum, making it one of the most important factors to compare when choosing a provider.
What is an interbank rate?
The interbank rate is the exchange rate at which large banks trade currencies with each other on wholesale markets. It is essentially equivalent to the mid-market rate. Retail customers do not access this rate directly — providers add a margin.
What is the best way to compare money transfer exchange rates?
The most reliable method is to enter your exact transfer amount on each platform and compare the net destination amount — the actual amount the recipient will receive. This captures both the rate margin and any fees in a single comparable figure.
Should I transfer during certain hours to get a better rate?
Exchange rates update continuously during trading hours. While market liquidity is generally higher during overlapping trading sessions (such as when London and New York are both open), predicting whether a rate will be better at a specific time is speculative. Focusing on provider selection and net payout comparison generally yields more reliable savings than timing the market.
What is a cross rate?
A cross rate is an exchange rate between two currencies calculated using a third intermediary currency — typically the US dollar. For example, if both GBP/USD and USD/INR rates are known, the GBP/INR cross rate can be derived. Most providers calculate cross rates automatically, but the process can introduce a slightly wider effective margin than trading a direct liquid pair.

Key Takeaways

Summary

Exchange rates are the most important variable in the cost of any international money transfer. Understanding how they work — and specifically how providers mark up rates beyond the mid-market benchmark — is fundamental to making informed decisions when sending money internationally.

  • The mid-market rate is the real market rate. You will generally not receive this rate — providers add a margin.
  • The spread is the provider's hidden margin. Always compare net payouts, not just stated fees.
  • Major currency pairs tend to have tighter spreads than minor or exotic pairs.
  • Spot rates apply to immediate transfers. Forward contracts lock in a rate for a future date.
  • Exchange rates are driven by interest rates, inflation, economic data, political events, and market sentiment.
  • For significant future currency needs, tools like forward contracts and limit orders may be worth exploring through specialist providers.
