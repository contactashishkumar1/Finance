Getting Started

Introduction

International money transfer is not a single uniform experience. The cost, speed, available delivery methods, provider options, and underlying banking infrastructure all differ depending on which two countries are involved in a transfer. What works perfectly for sending money from the UK to India may be completely different from what is needed for a transfer from Canada to the Philippines or the US to Mexico.

This hub is ArthCalculator's master directory for understanding money transfer by destination. It explains what corridors are, why they differ, and how to navigate the regional landscape. As individual country and corridor guide pages are published, they will be linked from this hub.

Core Concept

Understanding Transfer Corridors

A transfer corridor is defined by the sending and receiving country pair. Every unique combination of origin and destination country constitutes a corridor. The US-to-India corridor and the UK-to-India corridor are distinct corridors with different characteristics, pricing, and provider considerations — even though the destination country is the same.

Within a corridor, the following characteristics can vary:

💰 Cost structureCompetitive corridors with many providers tend to have lower effective costs. Corridors with fewer providers or lower transfer volumes may carry higher margins.
📦 Delivery methodsBank deposit, cash pickup, mobile wallet, and home delivery availability all depend on the destination country's infrastructure and provider partnerships.
⏱️ Transfer speedSettlement time depends on local banking hours, cut-off times, the number of correspondent banks, and whether local real-time payment infrastructure is available.
🏦 Banking requirementsThe banking identifiers needed — IBAN, SWIFT, routing number, IFSC, etc. — vary by destination country.
📋 Regulatory environmentCapital controls, reporting requirements, and transfer limits may apply in the sending or receiving country.
💱 Currency liquidityMore widely traded currencies have deeper liquidity and typically tighter exchange rate spreads. Less-traded currencies may carry wider margins.

Corridor maturity also matters: a mature corridor with established provider competition — such as US to Mexico — offers more choice, better pricing, and more delivery methods than a relatively underdeveloped corridor with fewer providers and less infrastructure.

Corridor Intelligence

Why Country Corridors Differ

The experience of sending money internationally is shaped by five interconnected factors that together define a corridor's character.

📊 Transfer Volume

The most frequently used corridors attract the most provider competition, which typically drives down pricing and improves delivery method availability. Corridors with very low transfer volumes may only be served by one or two providers, often at premium cost.

🏦 Banking Infrastructure

Countries with well-developed banking systems — a dense network of financial institutions, robust payment clearing systems, and high account penetration — enable faster and more reliable transfer settlement. Countries with limited formal banking infrastructure rely more on cash-based and mobile money delivery.

💱 Currency Characteristics

Currency liquidity affects pricing. Major currencies with deep global trading markets — USD, EUR, GBP, JPY — can be exchanged efficiently with tight spreads. Smaller, less-traded currencies require more operational effort and typically carry wider margins.

📋 Regulatory Environment

Regulatory requirements differ significantly by country. Some destinations impose limits on incoming foreign currency transfers, require specific reporting for amounts above a threshold, or restrict certain types of international payments. These requirements affect providers' ability to serve a corridor and can influence cost.

🤝 Correspondent Banking Relationships

The availability and quality of correspondent banking relationships between the sending and receiving country affects both routing cost and settlement time. Corridors with strong direct banking connections settle faster and with fewer intermediary deductions.

Region Overview

North America

North America is home to some of the world's highest-volume money transfer corridors, particularly those running between the United States and Latin America. The US-to-Mexico corridor is consistently among the largest in the world by transfer value, driven by a substantial diaspora community sending regular family support payments.

United States: As the world's largest remittance-sending country, the US sends funds to virtually every destination globally. It does not use IBAN. International wire transfers require SWIFT codes and routing numbers alongside account numbers. The US banking system uses ACH for domestic transfers and SWIFT for international wires. Multiple digital remittance providers have built their primary services around US outbound corridors.

Canada: Canada is a significant remittance sender with large South Asian and Southeast Asian diaspora communities. Like the US, Canada does not use IBAN. Canadian banks are well-connected to the global SWIFT network and generally well-supported by international transfer providers.

Mexico (as a destination): Mexico is one of the world's largest remittance recipients. The US-Mexico corridor is exceptionally competitive, with strong digital and traditional provider coverage, bank deposit to CLABE numbers, and widespread cash pickup through agent networks.

Region Overview

Europe

Europe is both a major sender and receiver of international transfers. The SEPA framework covers most of Europe for euro-denominated transfers, enabling simplified bank-to-bank payments between participating countries using IBAN and BIC. Cross-border transfers within the SEPA zone are generally fast, cost-effective, and well-supported.

United Kingdom: The UK is a major hub for international transfers, with large South Asian, African, and Eastern European diaspora communities. It uses sort codes and account numbers domestically, and IBANs + SWIFT codes for international transfers. The UK participates in SEPA despite having left the EU. The UK is home to multiple leading transfer providers and fintech companies.

European Union: EU member states use IBAN as the standard account identifier, and euro transfers within SEPA are fast and low-cost. Many digital transfer providers have their European headquarters in EU markets due to regulatory frameworks that enable pan-European financial services.

Eastern Europe: Countries such as Poland, Romania, and Ukraine are significant receivers of transfers from the Western European diaspora. Many Eastern European countries use IBAN and participate in SEPA.

Region Overview

Asia

Asia encompasses a diverse range of banking environments, from highly developed financial infrastructure in Singapore, Japan, and South Korea to emerging infrastructure in parts of South and Southeast Asia. Several of the world's largest remittance recipient countries are in this region.

India: India is one of the world's largest remittance recipients. It does not use IBAN — international transfers use SWIFT codes along with IFSC codes and account numbers. A wide range of providers support transfers to India across the US, UK, UAE, and other major corridors.

Philippines: The Philippines is another major remittance recipient, primarily from overseas Filipino workers. Cash pickup through local agents, bank deposit, and mobile wallet delivery are all well-supported. The PHP corridor is mature and highly competitive.

China: The China corridor is served by a specific set of providers due to regulatory requirements. Providers must comply with Chinese financial regulations, and not all international platforms support CNY transfers.

Bangladesh & Pakistan: Both are significant remittance destination countries, primarily receiving from the Middle East, UK, and other Gulf states. Mobile financial services have expanded transfer access in both markets.

Southeast Asia: Countries including Vietnam, Indonesia, Thailand, and Malaysia are growing remittance destinations. Mobile wallet delivery has expanded significantly in this region alongside bank deposit options.

Region Overview

Middle East

The Middle East — particularly the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries — is one of the world's largest remittance-sending regions. Large expatriate worker populations in the UAE, Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Qatar, Bahrain, and Oman send significant volumes of money to South Asia, Southeast Asia, and Africa.

UAE: The UAE uses IBAN (AE prefix) and is served by a wide range of both digital and traditional transfer providers. Exchange houses are a significant part of the remittance infrastructure in the UAE, offering in-person services for cash-based senders alongside digital channels.

Saudi Arabia: As the region's largest economy and home to a very large expatriate population, Saudi Arabia is a major remittance hub. Multiple providers operate in the KSA market, including local exchange companies and global digital platforms.

Kuwait, Qatar & Bahrain: Similar to the UAE and Saudi Arabia, these countries have large expatriate populations that send remittances across major South Asian and Southeast Asian corridors.

The Middle East to South Asia corridor — particularly UAE/Saudi to India/Pakistan/Bangladesh/Philippines — is among the highest-volume global corridors and features strong provider competition.

Region Overview

Africa

Africa presents a complex and diverse landscape for international money transfers. Intra-African transfers have historically faced above-average costs, partly due to currency liquidity constraints and correspondent banking friction. However, mobile money has profoundly transformed financial access across much of the continent.

East Africa: East Africa — particularly Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda, and Rwanda — is a global leader in mobile money adoption. Services have enabled millions of unbanked individuals to receive international transfers via mobile wallet, bypassing the need for a formal bank account. This region now has some of the most advanced mobile money ecosystems globally.

West Africa: Nigeria is Africa's largest remittance recipient by volume. Multiple providers serve the Nigeria corridor, including digital platforms and traditional agent networks. Ghana is also a significant receiver. West African currencies vary significantly in liquidity and exchange characteristics.

North Africa: Morocco, Egypt, and Tunisia are among the largest remittance recipients in North Africa, primarily from diaspora communities in Europe. IBAN is used in some North African countries. Morocco and Egypt have specific regulatory frameworks governing incoming transfers.

Sub-Saharan Africa (broader): This region often features above-average effective transfer costs due to currency complexity, limited correspondent banking density, and regulatory overhead. Competition has increased in recent years as digital providers have invested in African corridors.

Region Overview

Latin America

Latin America is a region of enormous remittance importance. Mexico, Guatemala, El Salvador, Honduras, Colombia, and the Dominican Republic are among the world's largest remittance recipients relative to GDP. The US-to-Latin America corridor cluster is among the most competitive globally.

Mexico: The US-Mexico corridor is one of the world's largest. Transfers use bank deposit to CLABE account numbers, cash pickup through an extensive agent network, and increasingly mobile wallet delivery. Competition is intense and effective costs have fallen significantly over time.

Central America: Guatemala, El Salvador, and Honduras are heavily dependent on remittances, primarily from the United States. Bank deposit and cash pickup are both well-established delivery methods. Mobile wallets are growing in availability.

South America: Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, and Brazil all receive meaningful remittance flows. Brazil has its own specific transfer infrastructure and currency regulations. Colombia and Peru are well-served by digital providers. Not all Latin American countries use IBAN — local account numbers are typically used alongside SWIFT codes for international transfers.

Currency volatility is a meaningful consideration in several Latin American markets, where local currencies can depreciate significantly, affecting the effective value of remittances received.

Region Overview

Oceania

Australia and New Zealand are the primary transfer hubs in Oceania. Both are significant remittance senders due to large South Asian, Southeast Asian, and Pacific Islander communities. Pacific Island nations are highly dependent on remittances relative to their national incomes.

Australia: Australia does not use IBAN. International transfers use SWIFT codes alongside BSB (Bank State Branch) codes and account numbers. Australia is well-served by digital transfer providers with strong coverage of major South Asian, Southeast Asian, and Pacific corridors. Australian fintech regulation has enabled a competitive digital transfer market.

New Zealand: Similar to Australia in terms of banking infrastructure and identifier requirements. New Zealand does not use IBAN. It has a large Polynesian community that sends remittances to Pacific Island nations.

Pacific Island nations: Samoa, Tonga, Fiji, Vanuatu, and others receive remittances that represent a very large proportion of GDP. The Pacific corridor has historically been one of the highest-cost remittance routes globally, though digital innovation and regulatory initiatives have helped reduce costs in some corridors.

Currency Factors

Currency Considerations by Destination

The destination currency significantly affects transfer pricing, availability, and the risk of exchange rate fluctuations affecting the value received.

Currency Type Characteristics Impact on Transfer
Major (USD, EUR, GBP, AUD) High liquidity, low volatility, widely traded Tight spreads, wide provider availability
Established EM (INR, PHP, MXN) Moderate liquidity, some volatility Competitive corridors, reasonable spreads
Less Liquid EM (GHS, NGN, PKR) Lower liquidity, higher volatility Wider spreads, fewer providers, higher cost
Restricted Currencies Capital controls or regulatory limits Limited providers, complex routing, possible delays

For corridors involving volatile currencies, the recipient's local currency value of a transfer can change significantly even between the time the transfer is initiated and when it arrives. Senders to high-volatility corridors may want to consider the timing of their transfers or use providers with competitive live rates.

How Funds Arrive

Delivery Methods by Destination Context

🏦 Bank Deposit

Funds transferred directly to the recipient's bank account. The most common delivery method for banked recipients. Requires a bank account and local clearing infrastructure. Settlement time varies from same-day to several business days depending on the corridor.

💵 Cash Pickup

The recipient collects cash at a designated agent location using a reference code or ID. Does not require a bank account. Particularly important for recipients in rural areas or countries with limited banking penetration. Settlement is typically very fast once the transfer is confirmed.

📱 Mobile Wallet

Funds delivered to a mobile money account registered to a phone number. Common in sub-Saharan Africa, South and Southeast Asia, and parts of Latin America. Allows unbanked recipients to receive international transfers without a formal bank account.

🏠 Home Delivery

Cash physically delivered to the recipient's address. Available in select corridors — particularly in some South and Southeast Asian markets. Less common than other delivery methods and may involve longer lead times.

Compliance & Rules

Regulatory Considerations

International money transfers are subject to regulation in both the sending and receiving country. Understanding general regulatory principles helps avoid delays and compliance issues.

Identity Verification (KYC)

All regulated providers are required to verify the identity of senders before processing international transfers. This typically involves providing government-issued photo identification and proof of address.

Source of Funds

Providers may request documentation showing the source of funds for larger or unusual transfers. This is a standard anti-money laundering requirement rather than a reason for concern for legitimate transfers.

Capital Controls

Some countries restrict the flow of foreign currency in or out. Capital controls can limit transfer amounts, require regulatory approval for large transfers, or restrict certain currencies. Providers are required to comply with applicable controls in each market.

Sanctions Screening

Providers are required to screen transfers against international sanctions lists. Transfers to certain countries, entities, or individuals may be blocked by applicable sanctions regimes.

Reporting Requirements

In the sending country, transfers above certain thresholds may be reported to financial intelligence authorities. This is a standard legal requirement and does not affect legitimate transfers.

Regulatory requirements evolve and can change with government policy. Users should verify current requirements with their chosen provider before initiating large or unusual transfers to or from specific corridors.

Avoid These

Common Mistakes in International Transfers

Not verifying the correct identifier for the destination country: Different countries require different banking identifiers. Using only a SWIFT code without an IBAN (for IBAN countries) or providing incorrect local account details can cause rejections or delays.
Assuming all providers cover your destination: Not every transfer service supports every destination country. Always verify that your chosen provider supports the specific corridor and delivery method your recipient needs before registering.
Ignoring currency volatility in the destination: In high-volatility corridors, the local currency value of a transfer can change significantly between initiation and arrival. For large transfers to volatile currency markets, consider timing or tools available to manage this risk.
Sending without verifying recipient details: Bank account numbers, mobile wallet numbers, and agent reference details should always be verified with the recipient before initiating a transfer.
Not comparing net payouts across providers: Transfer costs vary significantly across providers on the same corridor. Comparing the net amount the recipient will receive — not just stated fees — is the most reliable way to identify the most cost-effective option.
Failing to account for intermediary bank deductions on bank wires: Traditional bank wire transfers may be subject to SHA or BEN fee arrangements, meaning intermediary banks deduct fees as the transfer passes through. If the recipient must receive the full amount, clarify the fee arrangement before sending.

Common Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a money transfer corridor?
A money transfer corridor refers to a specific sending and receiving country pair. Every unique combination of origin and destination country constitutes a corridor with its own characteristics for pricing, delivery methods, speed, and provider availability.
Why do transfer costs vary by destination country?
Costs vary due to differences in local banking infrastructure, currency liquidity, provider competition, regulatory requirements, and the number of correspondent banks in the routing chain. High-volume corridors typically attract more competition and lower costs.
What delivery methods are available for international transfers?
Common delivery methods include bank deposit, cash pickup at agent locations, mobile wallet delivery, and in some corridors, home delivery. Availability depends on the specific destination country and the provider chosen.
Is bank deposit available in all destination countries?
No. Bank deposit requires the recipient to have a formal bank account and the destination to have accessible clearing infrastructure. In countries with limited banking penetration, cash pickup or mobile wallet may be the primary options.
What is mobile money and where is it commonly used?
Mobile money allows users to store and transfer value using a mobile phone number without a traditional bank account. It is widely used in sub-Saharan Africa, particularly East Africa, as well as parts of South and Southeast Asia and Latin America.
Does currency affect how I should choose a transfer provider?
Yes. Currency availability varies by provider. Major currencies are supported by almost all providers. Less common destination currencies may only be supported by specialist corridor providers with established local partnerships.
What are capital controls and how do they affect transfers?
Capital controls are restrictions imposed by a government on the flow of currency in or out of a country. They can limit transfer amounts, require regulatory approval for large transfers, or restrict certain currency flows. Providers must comply with applicable controls in each market.
What is the difference between a remittance and a wire transfer?
Remittance refers to money sent by a migrant worker back to their home country — typically for family support. A wire transfer is a broader term for any electronic transfer of funds between accounts. The two terms are sometimes used interchangeably but carry different connotations.
Which providers cover the most destination countries?
Established global agent networks such as Western Union and MoneyGram typically cover the largest number of countries for cash pickup. Digital platforms vary in corridor coverage — some specialise in high-volume routes while others prioritise breadth. Always verify on the provider's platform for your specific destination.
What is corridor maturity?
Corridor maturity refers to how well-developed a specific sending and receiving country pair is in terms of provider coverage, infrastructure, competitive pricing, and delivery method availability. Mature corridors offer more choice and better pricing than emerging ones.
Can I send money to an unbanked recipient in a rural area?
Yes, in many markets. Cash pickup at agent locations and mobile wallet delivery can serve recipients without formal bank accounts. Availability depends on the specific destination country and corridor.
What is a high-cost corridor and what causes it?
A high-cost corridor has above-average effective transfer costs. Causes include limited provider competition, low currency liquidity, complex correspondent banking chains, weak local banking infrastructure, and regulatory overhead.
How do exchange rates differ between corridors?
Exchange rate pricing varies by corridor based on the liquidity of the destination currency, provider competition, and local banking infrastructure. Major currency pairs typically have tighter spreads than exotic pairs.
Are there countries where international money transfers are restricted?
Some countries are subject to international sanctions that restrict financial flows. Providers are required by law to screen transactions against sanctions lists and may be unable to process transfers to certain destinations.
What factors should I consider when choosing a provider for a specific corridor?
Key factors include whether the provider covers your specific destination, available delivery methods, net payout in the destination currency, transfer speed, regulatory standing of the provider, and any minimum or maximum transfer limits.
What is the role of local agents in international money transfer?
Local agents — such as post offices, convenience stores, pharmacies, and dedicated transfer outlets — partner with transfer networks to offer cash pickup services. They are particularly important in markets where banking access is limited.
Which region has the lowest transfer costs on average?
Within SEPA (Europe), euro-denominated transfers are among the cheapest in the world due to the integrated payment framework. The South Asia corridor (US/UK/UAE to India or Pakistan) is also highly competitive due to transfer volume and provider density. Sub-Saharan African corridors have historically carried above-average costs.
How do I know which delivery method is best for my recipient?
The best delivery method depends on your recipient's circumstances. If they have a bank account, bank deposit is generally most convenient. If they use mobile money, wallet delivery is suitable. If they need cash immediately or have no bank/wallet, cash pickup is the most universal option.
What is a diaspora community and why does it matter for money transfer?
A diaspora community is a group of people who have moved from their country of origin to another country and maintain ties to their homeland. Diaspora communities are the primary drivers of remittance flows. Providers often develop specific services for high-diaspora corridors.

Key Takeaways

Summary

International money transfer is fundamentally a corridor-by-corridor experience. Understanding the specific characteristics of your sending and receiving country pair is the most important step in choosing the right provider and delivery method.

  • Every corridor — the combination of sending and receiving country — has unique pricing, delivery method availability, and banking infrastructure characteristics.
  • Banking infrastructure maturity determines whether bank deposit, mobile wallet, or cash pickup is the most practical delivery option for your recipient.
  • Currency liquidity affects exchange rate spreads — major currency pairs are cheaper to exchange than exotic or restricted currencies.
  • High-volume corridors such as US-Mexico, UK-India, and UAE-Philippines attract more competition and typically lower costs.
  • Mobile money has dramatically expanded financial access for unbanked recipients in sub-Saharan Africa and parts of Asia.
  • Regulatory requirements — including KYC, capital controls, and sanctions — vary by corridor and should be understood before initiating a large transfer.
